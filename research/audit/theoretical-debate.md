# Theoretical Debate — Deepened Analysis

## A. Classical Functionalism

Mitrany proposed function-specific international agencies addressing human needs, with technical self-determination determining scope and powers (Mitrany 1943, 1948). He was not genuinely apolitical: his work drew on pluralist political theory and aimed at undermining state sovereignty and nationalism (Mitrany and the Emergence of the Functional Approach, 2017). He differed from federalism by rejecting territorial reorganization of politics in favor of task-specific authority. The relationship between functional agencies, sovereignty, and peace rests on the claim that pooling sovereignty for specific tasks builds cross-border bonds that erode nationalism. This is an argument, not an empirically established causal law.

## B. Neofunctionalism

Functional spillover: cooperation in one sector creates functional demands in adjacent sectors. Political spillover: supranational officials expand scope. Cultivated spillover: interest groups mobilize around regional solutions. Spillback: reversal of integration. Conditions: pluralist interest representation, technocratic elites, economic interdependence, democratic accountability. Major criticisms: over-prediction of automaticity, elite bias, unfalsifiability, neglect of member-state preferences and exogenous shocks (Moravcsik 2005). Application outside Europe is contested; concepts may travel but mechanisms may differ.

## C. Chinese IR Scholarship

Relational theory (Qin Yaqing) emphasizes guanxi and relational processes over attribute-based analysis. Moral realism (Yan Xuetong) stresses moral norms and strategic leadership. Tianxia (Zhao Tingyang) proposes a world-embracing governance system. Social symbiosis (Ren Xiao) emphasizes coexistence. These are not functionalist: they are civilizational, relational, or moral in orientation. They should not be automatically classified as functionalist. Whether any can be integrated with functionalist concepts is an open question.

## D. Alternative Explanations

- Liberal institutionalism: institutions reduce transaction costs; assumes shared interests.
- Economic statecraft: cooperation as tool of state power and influence.
- Complex interdependence: multiple channels; minor role of force.
- Strategic regionalism: cooperation shaped by geopolitical competition.
- Developmental state: state-led development and industrial policy.
- Hegemonic order/power asymmetry: cooperation reflects and reinforces hierarchy.
- Constructivism: norms and identity shape interests.

Chinese Functionalism must demonstrate explanatory value beyond these frameworks, particularly in accounting for sectoral cooperation under asymmetry and smaller-state responses.
