# Independent Research Dossier 01
## China-ASEAN Economic Cooperation, Infrastructure, and the Philippine Case

**Prepared for:** Chinese Functionalism book project  
**Research date:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** Preliminary independent web research for integration and human review  
**Purpose:** Add substantive, traceable evidence to the working manuscript. This dossier is not a final literature review and does not constitute source approval.

---

## Executive summary

This dossier develops four empirical research areas that can materially improve the current manuscript: China-ASEAN trade, ACFTA 3.0, the China-Laos Railway, the Jakarta-Bandung High-Speed Railway, and Chinese development finance in the Philippines. It also adds two useful analytical studies: one on the relationship between political relations and China-ASEAN trade, and another on China's differentiated coercion and restraint towards the Philippines and Vietnam.

The evidence supports a more careful argument than the current draft offers. China-ASEAN economic cooperation is large, institutionalized, and increasingly embedded in regional supply chains. Yet these relationships are not simply harmonious, symmetrical, or insulated from political conflict. Trade interdependence can coexist with import concentration and unequal bargaining positions. Infrastructure may improve connectivity while producing uneven benefits and fiscal or governance risks. Development finance can produce aggregate economic associations without demonstrating that gains reach households evenly. Maritime disputes can persist alongside economic ties, and China's conduct towards Southeast Asian states can vary rather than follow a uniform pattern.

These findings strengthen the empirical case for studying functional cooperation under asymmetry. They do **not**, by themselves, establish Chinese Functionalism as a distinct theory. Existing approaches such as economic statecraft, liberal intergovernmentalism, complex interdependence, development finance, and regional political economy remain plausible explanations.

## 1. China-ASEAN trade: interdependence is growing, but it is not automatically symmetrical

### 1.1 Official trade picture

ASEAN's official economic-relations page reports that China has been ASEAN's largest trading partner since 2009, while ASEAN has been China's largest trading partner since 2019. Two-way merchandise trade reached USD 772.4 billion in 2024, equivalent to 20.1 percent of ASEAN's total trade. Chinese foreign direct investment into ASEAN reached USD 19.3 billion in 2024, making China the third-largest source of FDI among ASEAN dialogue partners that year. These are official ASEAN figures and should be used with the stated definitions and period, rather than mixed casually with Chinese customs figures or other datasets.

Source: ASEAN Secretariat, *ASEAN-China Economic Relations* ([official page](https://asean.org/our-communities/economic-community/integration-with-global-economy/asean-china-economic-relation/)).

ASEAN's 2025 merchandise-trade dataset is available through ASEANstats, with a stated data update of 1 July 2026 and 2025 observations included where available. This is the preferred source for building a consistent time series because it identifies the statistical domain as international merchandise trade statistics and provides country and partner filters. Before using a 2025 total, the research team should export the ASEAN reporter-China partner series directly from ASEANstats and document the extraction date and any missing observations.

Source: ASEANstats, *Trade in Goods (IMTS), Annually* ([data portal](https://data.aseanstats.org/trade-annually)).

### 1.2 The distribution of trade flows matters

A September 2026 RSIS commentary by Ang Siah Hwee reports that ASEAN exports to China increased from USD 208.2 billion in 2014 to USD 388.7 billion in 2023, while imports from China increased from USD 272.0 billion to USD 526.5 billion. It reports that since 2020 China has accounted for more than 20 percent of ASEAN's merchandise exports and more than 30 percent of its merchandise imports. The figures suggest substantial and deepening integration, while also showing that aggregate import exposure has grown faster than exports over the period covered.

These numbers should not be treated as a complete measure of dependence. Aggregate trade values do not show the composition of goods, the extent to which imports are intermediate inputs used by ASEAN manufacturers, the distribution of gains within member states, or the availability of alternative suppliers. They do, however, justify a more precise examination of supply-chain concentration and trade asymmetry.

Source: Ang, S. H. (2026, September 24), *Can ASEAN Help Mitigate Other Countries' Value-Chain Dependence on China?*, RSIS ([article](https://rsis.edu.sg/rsis-publication/rsis/can-asean-help-mitigate-other-countries-value-chain-dependence-on-china/)). This is an expert commentary, not a peer-reviewed journal article. Use it as an analytical source and trace its statistical claims to the underlying trade datasets before final publication.

### 1.3 Trade is affected by political relations

Wang and Tao (2024) use a gravity model and event data from the Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone to examine China-ASEAN bilateral trade between January 2001 and December 2020. Their study analyses 1,204,126 event records and concludes that improvements in bilateral relations are associated with higher bilateral trade. The authors also report that China's export trade is more sensitive to bilateral relations than its import trade, and that China's responses to ASEAN states have a greater effect on bilateral trade than the reverse direction.

This is especially relevant to the book because it connects economic exchange to political relations rather than treating trade as an autonomous technical domain. It does not prove that political relations cause all observed trade movements, nor does it establish a functionalist process. The article's model, event coding, country heterogeneity, and robustness checks should be examined before the study is used for a strong causal claim.

Source: Wang, Y., & Tao, Y. (2024). The effect of fluctuations in bilateral relations on trade: Evidence from China and ASEAN countries. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 11*, Article 32. [https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-023-02525-w](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-023-02525-w). Open-access article; initial review here is based on the publisher abstract and indexed record, not a complete full-text review.

### Implication for the manuscript

The trade section should not say simply that increasing trade demonstrates mutually beneficial functional cooperation. It should distinguish at least four propositions:

1. Trade volumes and institutional arrangements show the scale of economic interaction.
2. Import and export composition indicate the direction and degree of asymmetry.
3. Supply-chain interdependence may create shared interests but also vulnerabilities.
4. Political relations can influence the conditions under which trade expands or contracts.

This makes the trade case useful for testing how cooperation, interdependence, and power interact. It does not make trade growth proof of a distinct Chinese Functionalism.

## 2. ACFTA 3.0: institutional innovation, but legal status must be stated accurately

The ASEAN-China Free Trade Area (ACFTA) developed through a series of instruments covering trade in goods, services, and investment. ASEAN records the initial framework agreement in 2002, the goods agreement in 2004, the services agreement in 2007, and the investment agreement in 2009. Subsequent upgrades broadened commitments and revised rules of origin.

The ACFTA 3.0 Upgrade Protocol was signed on 28 October 2025. Its subject matter extends beyond traditional tariff liberalisation and includes digital economy, green economy, supply-chain connectivity, competition, consumer protection, standards, services, investment, micro, small and medium-sized enterprises, economic and technical cooperation, and dispute settlement.

**Important current-status correction:** The ASEAN Legal Instruments portal currently lists the ACFTA 3.0 Upgrade Protocol as **Not In Force**. Its ratification/acceptance/approval table lists instruments from Brunei Darussalam, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, Singapore, Thailand, and Viet Nam, but the page still shows the protocol as not in force. Singapore's Enterprise Singapore page likewise describes it as signed and pending ratification by the parties. The manuscript must therefore distinguish signature, ratification/acceptance, entry into force, and actual implementation. It should not describe ACFTA 3.0 as an already fully implemented regime.

Sources:
- ASEAN Legal Instruments, *ACFTA 3.0 Upgrade Protocol* ([official status page](https://dev.agreement.asean.org/dialogue-partners/framework-agreement-on-comprehensive-economic-co-operation-between-asean-and-the-peoples-republic-of-china/protocol-to-further-upgrade-the-framework-agreement-on-comprehensive-economic-co-operation-and-certain-agreements-thereunder-between-the-association-of-southeast-asian-nations-and-the-peoples/)).
- Enterprise Singapore, *ASEAN-China Free Trade Area* ([official agreement summary](https://www.enterprisesg.gov.sg/grow-your-business/go-global/international-agreements/find-an-fta/acfta)).
- ASEAN Secretariat, *Priority Areas of Cooperation* ([official page](https://asean.org/our-communities/economic-community/integration-with-global-economy/asean-china-economic-relation/priority-areas-of-cooperation/)).

### Analytical significance

ACFTA 3.0 is a useful example of the expansion of functional cooperation into newer regulatory and technical fields. It can support analysis of institutional design and the widening scope of economic cooperation. It cannot yet support a claim that all the new commitments have produced practical outcomes. The book should ask whether the protocol's broader agenda creates new routines and institutional linkages, whether implementation is uneven across member states, and how the rules distribute adjustment costs and benefits.

## 3. China-Laos Railway: strong connectivity effects, uneven reach, and conditional developmental gains

### 3.1 What was expected before operation

The World Bank's 2020 report, *Transforming Lao PDR from a Land-locked to a Land-linked Economy*, treated the railway as a potentially transformative corridor, but repeatedly made its benefits conditional on complementary reforms. The 414-kilometre Vientiane-Boten section was expected to improve access to Chinese and wider regional markets. The report estimated that, with the appropriate reforms, Lao PDR's aggregate income could increase by up to 21 percent over the long term. It also described the project's USD 5.9 billion construction cost as roughly one-third of Lao GDP in 2017. The ownership structure of the railway company was described as 30 percent Lao and 70 percent Chinese. The report stressed the importance of cargo and passenger demand, logistics services, road links, border clearance, and trade facilitation.

These were ex-ante projections and policy conditions, not observed results. They should be presented as expectations at the planning stage.

Source: World Bank. (2020). *Transforming Lao PDR from a land-locked to a land-linked economy*. [World Bank publication page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/lao/publication/transforming-lao-pdr-from-a-land-locked-to-a-land-linked-economy).

### 3.2 A contrasting ex-ante assessment

Lane (2020), in an Asian Development Bank Institute working paper, examined the approximately USD 6 billion Lao PDR-China railway through two modelling approaches and comparison with a similar investment programme in Mongolia. The paper concluded that the project was unlikely to bring major economic benefits under its assumptions and could create a large contingent liability for Laos. This is a model-based assessment published before the railway began operation. It is not evidence that the railway later failed, but it is an important counterpoint to more optimistic projections.

Source: Lane, J. A. (2020). *Reevaluating the economic benefits of the Lao People's Democratic Republic (Lao PDR)-People's Republic of China high-speed rail and its implications for fiscal stability of the Lao PDR* (ADBI Working Paper No. 1181). Asian Development Bank Institute. [Publication page](https://www.adb.org/publications/reevaluating-economic-benefits-lao-prc-high-speed-rail).

### 3.3 Early post-opening evidence

The World Bank's FY2024 reporting on its Laos railway analytical work states that, two years after launch, passenger and freight demand exceeded forecasts, with freight accounting for most railway revenue despite operational challenges. It reports that the railway improved Lao firms' access to Chinese markets through faster and more reliable freight transport. However, the documented exporting base benefiting at that early stage was narrow: approximately a dozen exporting firms and products, overwhelmingly iron ore, with some timber, rubber, and agricultural commodities. More than 200 importers had used the railway for Chinese supplies. The World Bank also emphasizes that benefits were uneven across sectors, regions, and firms, and that coordination failures and access barriers limited the potential for broader gains.

This is one of the most valuable findings for the book. It avoids the false choice between saying the railway is a success or a failure. The railway can be operational and commercially useful while the distribution and breadth of developmental benefits remain limited.

Source: World Bank, *Annual Report 2024*, section “Railway and Economic Development: Evidence from Laos” ([report PDF](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/b97f130903c3b38cddbdbabd47c63661-0060072025/original/UFT-Annual-Report-2024.pdf)). The finding is summarized in the World Bank's published report. The underlying study, *Preliminary evidence on the economic impact of the Laos-China Railway*, should be located and reviewed directly before a detailed chapter citation is finalized.

### 3.4 More recent operator-reported volumes

A report carried by China's State Council portal in July 2026, citing China Railway Kunming Group, states that cross-border freight on the railway exceeded 20 million tonnes since opening in December 2021, with more than 23,000 cross-border freight train trips. The report says the cumulative volume exceeded 5 million tonnes by the end of 2023 and 10 million tonnes by the end of 2024, and that goods categories expanded to more than 3,800. These are operator-reported figures. They indicate the scale of corridor use, but they should be independently triangulated and should not be treated as direct measures of Lao welfare, domestic value added, or equitable benefit distribution.

Source: State Council of the People's Republic of China / Xinhua. (2026, July 8). *China-Laos Railway transports over 20 million tonnes of cargo*. [Official news report](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202607/08/content_WS6a4e3717c6d00ca5f9a0c11c.html).

### Analytical significance

The railway is a strong case for examining functional cooperation because it provides a concrete transport service that can reduce trade frictions and create new market connections. Yet its developmental effect depends on domestic logistics, complementary roads, firms' ability to export, border administration, and the distribution of access. It also embeds an asymmetric ownership and financing relationship. The case therefore allows the manuscript to distinguish infrastructure output (a completed and operating railway), utilization (freight and passenger demand), economic outcomes (firm access, exports, jobs, income), and distribution (which firms, sectors, and regions benefit).

## 4. Jakarta-Bandung High-Speed Railway: risk is negotiated, not simply calculated

Wijaya (2024) provides a peer-reviewed, open-access analysis of the Jakarta-Bandung High-Speed Railway. Rather than treating risk as a purely technical problem, the article examines how Chinese companies managed risk by building alliances with influential Indonesian political and economic actors. It uses a Gramscian account of the state and social forces to explain compromises among state capital, internationalized state institutions, and industrial capital. The article argues that the management of project risk became embedded in the Indonesian state and its infrastructure regime.

This analysis adds something important to the manuscript's treatment of power asymmetry. The recipient state is not merely a passive borrower, and the Chinese side is not acting in a political vacuum. Project outcomes are shaped through domestic coalitions, negotiations, institutional choices, and compromises. This is more analytically useful than simply labelling the project either a Chinese strategic success or an Indonesian debt problem.

The article's full-text record identifies it as peer reviewed and open access. It was published in *Journal of Contemporary Asia*, pages 405-429, with DOI 10.1080/00472336.2024.2378856. A detailed source review should inspect the article itself and distinguish Wijaya's argument from other empirical accounts of project costs, ridership, and financial performance.

Source: Wijaya, T. (2024). “Risk is not measured, but contested and compromised”: A case study of Jakarta-Bandung high-speed railway. *Journal of Contemporary Asia*, 54(3), 405-429. [https://doi.org/10.1080/00472336.2024.2378856](https://doi.org/10.1080/00472336.2024.2378856). Open-access record and author manuscript available through [Murdoch University Research Portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Risk-is-not-Measured-but-Contested/991005690069907891).

A separate Indonesian study by Putri and Mahani (2024) models the project's financial viability under different ridership scenarios. Its abstract reports that cost escalation and delay reduced the project's modelled internal rate of return below the minimum attractive rate of return and made net present value negative under the revised inputs. The study's outputs are scenario-dependent and should not be treated as a definitive audited financial result. It is a useful supplementary source, not a substitute for the project's audited accounts and operating data.

Source: Putri, A. A., & Mahani, I. (2024). Dampak finansial akibat keterlambatan pelaksanaan proyek konstruksi kereta cepat Jakarta-Bandung. *Journal of Civil Engineering, Building and Transportation, 8*(2). [https://doi.org/10.31289/jcebt.v7i2.10195](https://doi.org/10.31289/jcebt.v7i2.10195).

### Analytical significance

This case can examine how functional cooperation is politically constructed and maintained. The railway is a transport solution, but its design, financing, risk allocation, and implementation are negotiated through domestic political-economic structures. This may be better explained by political economy and state-business relations than by functionalism alone. The book should use the case comparatively, not assume that a completed railway necessarily demonstrates successful integration or mutual benefit.

## 5. The Philippine case: development finance, project failure, and political limits

### 5.1 Scale and composition of Chinese development finance

Custer, Burgess, Solis, Sritharan, and Mathew (2024), in AidData's *Beijing's Big Bet on the Philippines*, track approximately USD 9.1 billion in Chinese state-directed development-finance commitments for more than 200 Philippine projects from 2000 to 2022. Their analysis draws on AidData's Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset, OECD Creditor Reporting System data, supplemental desk research, and project-level information. The authors distinguish Official Development Assistance, which includes grants and no- or low-interest loans, from Other Official Flows, which include loans and export credits closer to market terms. They report that only around 6 percent of Chinese financing commitments in their dataset were on grant or highly concessional terms.

The report also places China in comparative perspective. It estimates USD 9.1 billion in Chinese commitments over the period, compared with USD 21.7 billion from Japan, USD 23.8 billion from the Asian Development Bank, and USD 16.3 billion from the World Bank. This comparison must be presented carefully because donor instruments, reporting coverage, concessionality, and project types are not identical. The report itself cautions against simplistic donor comparisons.

Source: Custer, S., Burgess, B., Solis, J. A., Sritharan, N., & Mathew, D. (2024). *Beijing's big bet on the Philippines: Decoding two decades of China's financing for development*. AidData at William & Mary. [Full report](https://docs.aiddata.org/reports/beijings-big-bet-on-the-philippines/full-report.html); [publication page](https://www.aiddata.org/publications/beijings-big-bet-on-the-philippines-decoding-two-decades-of-chinas-financing-for-development); [dataset](https://www.aiddata.org/data/beijings-big-bet-on-the-philippines-decoding-two-decades-of-chinas-financing-for-development-v-1-0).

### 5.2 Commitments are not the same as disbursements or completed projects

A key methodological point is that the USD 9.1 billion figure refers to commitments in a project-level financing dataset, not a simple measure of cash already disbursed or completed assets. The AidData report separately examines whether Beijing followed through on commitments, implementation risks, project cancellation, and downstream outcomes. The manuscript should preserve these distinctions.

The report documents six suspended or cancelled projects with different explanations. It links Northrail and the National Broadband Network to weak management and oversight, contractual and financial problems, corruption allegations, and legal or administrative intervention. It links the Agus 3 Hydropower and Cyber Education projects to local opposition over project goals, financing, or potential harm. It identifies COVID-19 and resource allocation as reasons for China's withdrawal from the Panay-Guimaras-Negros Island Bridges project, while noting that geopolitical sensitivities and maritime disputes affected calls to cancel some Chinese contracts. These cases should not be flattened into one explanation such as “Chinese coercion,” “debt trap,” or “Philippine mismanagement.” The causes differed by project and included both supply-side decisions and recipient-country institutions.

The report also gives concrete project-level detail. Northrail encountered delays, rising costs, disputed implementation arrangements, displacement concerns, and litigation. The NBN project, financed by China Eximbank and implemented by ZTE, was cancelled after corruption allegations and governance problems. Agus 3 was suspended after the financing agreement, with government approval and local opposition among the relevant factors. The Panay-Guimaras-Negros bridges were suspended before funds were disbursed and later pursued with South Korean participation. These cases provide negative evidence about the conversion of announced cooperation into completed outputs.

### 5.3 Aggregate economic outcomes and household experience

AidData's statistical analysis reports positive associations between Chinese development finance and Chinese FDI, on the one hand, and Philippine GDP, GDP per capita, and GNI, on the other. It also reports a negative association between increases in these financing measures and survey responses about the ability to afford food and shelter. The associations with the local job climate and confidence in the local economy were positive but not statistically significant in the reported analysis. The authors expressly caution that the diverse nature of the projects makes it difficult to establish that Chinese investment alone caused particular outcomes.

This is a useful finding for a book concerned with functional cooperation and welfare. Aggregate economic performance and household-level experience can diverge. The manuscript must not present the statistical associations as causal proof that Chinese financing either improved or worsened household welfare.

The report was prepared by AidData at William & Mary in collaboration with Asia Society Philippines and states that its research received support from the United States Department of State. Its authors state that the findings and conclusions are their own and do not necessarily reflect the views of funders or partners. This funding disclosure should be noted in the source assessment, alongside the report's dataset, methods, transparency, and limitations. It is a reason for transparent source evaluation and triangulation, not grounds by itself to dismiss the report.

### Analytical significance

The Philippine case allows the manuscript to test the limits of functional cooperation across several levels:

- Bilateral policy commitments may be announced but not implemented.
- Financing can be accepted by recipient institutions, but domestic governance and local opposition can interrupt projects.
- Economic cooperation can continue even as maritime disputes worsen.
- Changes in diplomatic relations can affect the volume and direction of cooperation.
- Aggregate indicators may not capture how individual communities experience projects.

These are strong reasons to examine smaller-state agency, domestic politics, implementation, and distribution, not simply the size of Chinese financing commitments.

## 6. Maritime competition and differentiated treatment of smaller states

Velasco (2026), in an open-access article in the *Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs*, compares China's conduct towards the Philippines and Vietnam in the South China Sea. Drawing on maritime incident data and elite-level interactions, the article argues that China has used more coercive tactics towards the Philippines while showing greater restraint towards Vietnam. It attributes this variation to the interaction of perceived threat-enhancing mechanisms, including alliance ties and legal internationalization, and affinity-enhancing mechanisms, including party-to-party ties and crisis-management channels.

The article's value for this project is not that its explanation must be accepted. It provides a recent, directly relevant competing framework for explaining why China behaves differently towards two Southeast Asian claimant states. It also cautions against treating “China” or “ASEAN” as unitary actors whose interactions follow one regional pattern. The author's argument should be compared with realist, constructivist, alliance-politics, and domestic-politics explanations, and its incident selection and causal inference should be critically reviewed.

Source: Velasco, J. C. (2026). Coercion and restraint in the South China Sea: China's differentiated assertiveness towards the Philippines and Vietnam. *Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs*. Published online 27 February 2026. [https://doi.org/10.1177/18681034261427457](https://doi.org/10.1177/18681034261427457). Open access.

## 7. What this evidence does to the Chinese Functionalism argument

The evidence supports a descriptive claim: China and Southeast Asian states participate in extensive sectoral and institutional cooperation across trade, infrastructure, transport, technology, and development finance. The evidence also supports a second descriptive claim: these cooperative arrangements operate under unequal economic and political conditions, and their effects vary across states, sectors, firms, and communities.

The stronger theoretical claim remains unproven. The existence of cooperation under asymmetry does not, by itself, establish a distinct Chinese Functionalism. The cases can be explained in substantial part through established approaches:

- **Liberal institutionalism and intergovernmentalism** help explain negotiated agreements, tariff rules, institutional routines, and state bargaining.
- **Economic statecraft** helps explain the strategic use of trade, loans, market access, and infrastructure finance.
- **Complex interdependence** helps explain multiple channels of interaction and mutual vulnerability, while requiring attention to unequal sensitivity and vulnerability.
- **Developmental-state and political-economy approaches** help explain infrastructure planning, financing, domestic coalitions, and implementation.
- **Realist and constructivist approaches** help explain variation in maritime coercion, alliance signalling, elite trust, and political affinity.

A useful provisional contribution for Chinese Functionalism may therefore be narrower: an analytical lens for examining the interaction between sector-specific problem-solving, institutionalization, asymmetric resource relations, and recipient-state agency. To move beyond description, the framework would need to identify a mechanism or sequence that existing theories do not explain as well. For instance, it may ask under what conditions sectoral problem-solving creates durable cross-border routines, expands into related policy domains, changes domestic actor incentives, or produces political spillover. The evidence must also allow for spillback, resistance, stalled implementation, coercion, and project exit.

The project should not claim that these mechanisms are uniquely Chinese until comparative evidence establishes that claim. A careful book may conclude that Chinese Functionalism is best treated as a heuristic or analytical adaptation, or that it adds too little beyond existing theory. Either conclusion is academically legitimate.

## 8. Recommended revisions to the current manuscript

1. **Update ACFTA 3.0's legal status.** Describe the protocol as signed in October 2025 but still listed as not in force by ASEAN's Legal Instruments portal as of the research date. Do not describe it as fully implemented.
2. **Replace vague trade claims with clearly defined figures.** Use ASEAN's 2024 merchandise-trade and FDI figures as the principal official baseline. If using 2025 preliminary figures, identify them as preliminary and document the extraction source and date.
3. **Add a trade asymmetry discussion.** Include the growth of both exports and imports, and explain why gross trade values do not equal dependence or net welfare.
4. **Develop the China-Laos Railway as a before-and-after case.** Contrast pre-opening forecasts and debt concerns with early operational evidence. Separate utilization from broad-based development outcomes.
5. **Rewrite the Jakarta-Bandung case around political economy and risk allocation.** Use Wijaya (2024) as a central analytical source and add independent operating and financial data.
6. **Substantially expand the Philippine case.** Use AidData's project-level evidence, but disclose methodology, commitment-versus-disbursement distinctions, funding source, and causal limitations.
7. **Add differentiated maritime behaviour.** Consider Velasco (2026) as a competing explanation for differences between Philippine and Vietnamese experiences.
8. **Make the theory comparison explicit in each case.** Ask what functionalism identifies, then ask whether economic statecraft, intergovernmentalism, interdependence, or political economy explains the same observation more convincingly.
