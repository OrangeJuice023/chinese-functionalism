# Chinese Functionalism: Independent Research Dossier 03

## Maritime Cooperation, the South China Sea, and the Limits of Functional Cooperation

**Research date:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** Independent research memo for integration into the Chinese Functionalism project  
**Purpose:** Expand the empirical and theoretical treatment of maritime cooperation, disputes, maritime law, and the limits of sectoral cooperation in Southeast Asia.

---

## Executive Summary

The South China Sea is an important empirical setting for evaluating the proposed Chinese Functionalism framework because it contains both sustained efforts at practical cooperation and persistent, sometimes dangerous, strategic conflict. ASEAN and China have developed recurring diplomatic mechanisms under the 2002 Declaration on the Conduct of Parties in the South China Sea (DOC), including meetings, workshops, training, and consultations. The DOC expressly identifies marine environmental protection, marine scientific research, navigation safety, search and rescue, and efforts against transnational crime as possible areas for cooperation while disputes remain unresolved. ASEAN also identifies these domains as practical cooperation priorities.

These arrangements show that functional cooperation can be pursued without first resolving sovereignty disputes. Yet the evidence does not demonstrate that such cooperation has produced a durable settlement, an effective regional maritime regime, or automatic spillover into political integration. The Code of Conduct (COC) negotiations remain ongoing as of 1 November 2026. Philippine officials have expressed optimism about concluding the process by the end of 2026, but publicly available information still describes negotiations over legal nature, geographic scope, third-party rights, enforcement, and the relationship between the COC and the DOC.

The Philippines-China relationship offers a more focused example. The Bilateral Consultation Mechanism (BCM), established in 2017, created a channel for managing disputes and discussing possible practical cooperation. However, an ISEAS analysis of the January 2024 meeting found that the two sides had not reached agreement on concrete projects, particularly on oil and gas cooperation. The July 2024 provisional understanding on Philippine rotation and resupply missions to BRP Sierra Madre at Second Thomas Shoal appears to have reduced immediate operational friction for a period. According to an Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative (AMTI) analysis, 13 missions took place between the arrangement and 4 March 2026 without dangerous encounters, according to Philippine officials. That limited operational accommodation should not be mistaken for a broader settlement. Philippine authorities subsequently reported interference with a resupply mission on 24 September 2026, while earlier September statements described other confrontations and incidents. These reports must be attributed to the reporting institution and, where possible, checked against the other party's account.

The 2016 South China Sea Arbitral Award is essential to the legal context. The tribunal did not decide sovereignty over land features or delimit a maritime boundary. It did interpret and apply the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) to questions concerning historic rights, maritime entitlements, feature status, fishing rights, environmental harm, and certain state conduct. The tribunal found that several Chinese actions violated the Philippines' sovereign rights or China's environmental obligations under UNCLOS. China rejects the award and states that it neither accepts nor recognizes it. A rigorous manuscript should accurately explain both the tribunal's legal conclusions and China's public position without implying that the tribunal decided questions it expressly left open.

The most important theoretical implication is that maritime cooperation appears capable of being compartmentalized. States can maintain dialogue, conduct limited practical arrangements, or cooperate in technical areas while continuing to disagree over sovereignty, jurisdiction, and maritime entitlements. This is relevant to functionalist analysis, but it is not evidence of automatic spillover. The case may instead show bounded cooperation, tactical accommodation, and institutionalized dispute management under conditions of continuing competition. Economic statecraft, bargaining theories, international law, and security studies remain necessary competing explanations.

For the Chinese Functionalism project, the maritime evidence should therefore be used as a hard test rather than as a showcase of successful functional integration. It can help identify the conditions under which practical cooperation survives conflict, the limits of technical cooperation when core interests are contested, and the ways smaller states use institutions, law, diplomacy, public communication, and external partnerships to preserve agency.

## 1. Why the Maritime Domain Matters to This Project

The book's central question concerns the relationship between sectoral cooperation and power asymmetry. Maritime affairs make that relationship particularly visible. The South China Sea contains shared practical problems, including navigation safety, search and rescue, marine pollution, fisheries management, marine scientific research, and transnational crime. These issues can create functional reasons for cooperation among governments even when they disagree over sovereignty and jurisdiction.

At the same time, the maritime domain is not politically neutral. Coast guards, maritime law enforcement agencies, naval forces, fishing fleets, maritime militia, survey vessels, and offshore energy interests operate in contested spaces. A technical or operational issue can therefore become entangled with jurisdictional claims and strategic signaling. Search and rescue, fisheries protection, or marine research may appear function-specific, but the location, participants, data, and rules governing these activities can carry political significance.

This makes the maritime domain useful for distinguishing three propositions that should not be conflated:

1. **Functional cooperation exists.** Governments have agreed to pursue practical cooperation and have established dialogue and consultation mechanisms.
2. **Functional cooperation produces measurable results.** This requires evidence of implementation and outcomes, not merely agreements, meetings, or declarations.
3. **Functional cooperation transforms political relations.** This is a stronger claim requiring evidence that practical cooperation generates durable trust, institutional binding, changed preferences, reduced conflict, or broader cooperation.

The available evidence supports the first proposition. It offers some limited examples relevant to the second, such as a provisional operational arrangement for resupply missions. It does not establish the third as a general pattern.

## 2. The 2002 Declaration on the Conduct of Parties

The DOC, adopted by ASEAN member states and China in Phnom Penh on 4 November 2002, is the central political instrument for understanding the regional cooperation framework. It affirms commitments to peaceful settlement, self-restraint, and confidence-building while disputes remain unresolved.

Paragraph 6 is especially relevant to functional cooperation. It states that, pending a comprehensive and durable settlement, the parties concerned may explore or undertake cooperative activities. The listed areas include:

- marine environmental protection;
- marine scientific research;
- safety of navigation and communication at sea;
- search and rescue operations; and
- combating transnational crime, including drug trafficking, piracy, armed robbery at sea, and illegal arms trafficking.

The paragraph also states that the modalities, scope, and locations of bilateral and multilateral cooperation should be agreed by the parties concerned before implementation. This qualification matters. The DOC does not create a ready-made regional authority that can impose projects or procedures. It establishes a political basis for exploring cooperation, while leaving important decisions to the parties concerned.

The DOC is a political declaration rather than a comprehensive, legally binding dispute-settlement treaty. It does not resolve the underlying territorial and maritime disputes. Its language creates space for cooperation while preserving the parties' positions. This arrangement resembles a practical functional approach in one respect: cooperation can be organized around specific shared needs without requiring prior political settlement. However, the requirement for agreement among the parties and the persistence of sovereignty claims also demonstrate the limits of technical autonomy.

The DOC's significance should not be overstated. The existence of a list of cooperative fields does not prove that every field has produced sustained joint activity or measurable outcomes. ASEAN's public portal describes workshops, training, and seminars under the DOC Work Plan in areas such as environmental protection, marine science, navigation safety, search and rescue, and transnational crime. These are evidence of dialogue and capacity-building activities. The public material reviewed for this dossier does not provide a consolidated evaluation of their outcomes, continuity, cost, or impact on maritime behavior.

## 3. The Code of Conduct Negotiations in 2026

The COC process is an important institutional test. The DOC was intended to help build confidence and provide a basis for subsequent negotiations. ASEAN and China began the present COC negotiation process in 2018, following earlier political commitments to work toward a code. The negotiations have continued for years, which itself demonstrates that institutionalized dialogue can survive persistent disagreement. It also demonstrates that such dialogue does not automatically resolve the underlying issues.

There were several official milestones in 2026:

- On 30 January, the 25th ASEAN-China Senior Officials' Meeting on the Implementation of the DOC took place in Cebu. The participants discussed implementation of the DOC, marine environmental protection, scientific research, search and rescue, law enforcement, and progress toward a COC.
- On 21 May, the 26th Senior Officials' Meeting took place in Kuala Lumpur. ASEAN and China reaffirmed the DOC and discussed maintaining momentum toward a COC.
- On 19 to 21 August, the 57th ASEAN-China Joint Working Group on the Implementation of the DOC met in Manila to advance COC consultations.
- In July, Philippine Foreign Affairs Secretary Ma. Theresa Lazaro stated that ASEAN and China aimed to conclude the COC by the end of 2026. She identified continuing discussions over the COC's geographic scope, legal nature, relationship with the DOC, and rights of third parties.
- On 8 October, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. again described the negotiations as making good progress, while acknowledging that disagreements over wording and definitions remained.

As of the research cut-off of 1 November 2026, the COC should be described as under negotiation, not as concluded, signed, or in force. The end-of-year target is a political objective, not a completed outcome. The legal force of the eventual document, its geographic scope, enforcement mechanisms, relationship to UNCLOS, and treatment of third-party states remain analytically important questions.

For the book, the COC process provides evidence of sustained institutional engagement and negotiation. It does not yet provide evidence that an effective regional maritime governance regime has been completed. It may ultimately strengthen regional rules, but this should not be assumed before the text, legal status, implementation procedures, and subsequent behavior can be assessed.

## 4. ASEAN's Practical Maritime Cooperation Agenda

ASEAN identifies a broad agenda for maritime cooperation. The ASEAN Maritime Forum has discussed maritime connectivity, maritime security and safety, search and rescue, sea-lane security, maritime domain awareness, marine ecosystems, biodiversity, aquatic resources, and ecotourism. Under the DOC framework, ASEAN and China have also identified environmental protection, marine scientific research, navigation safety, search and rescue, and transnational crime as potential areas of practical cooperation.

This agenda supports an important distinction between regional functional needs and the political conditions of their implementation. Marine pollution, shipping safety, fisheries depletion, and search and rescue are not confined to a single national jurisdiction. They create practical incentives for coordination. However, joint action requires agreement on access, data sharing, operational authority, funding, and the geographic area in which activities may occur. In contested waters, even the designation of a project location can imply a position on jurisdiction.

The public institutional material reviewed for this dossier documents recurring meetings, workshops, training, and broad priorities. It does not establish the scale or effectiveness of sustained joint programs in each field. Future research should locate project-level records, budgets, participant lists, monitoring reports, scientific outputs, and evaluations. Until then, the manuscript should describe these activities as institutionalized cooperation initiatives and avoid calling them successful regional management regimes.

## 5. The Philippines-China Bilateral Consultation Mechanism

The Philippines and China established the Bilateral Consultation Mechanism on the South China Sea in 2017. The mechanism was intended to provide a regular channel for discussing maritime concerns, reducing misunderstanding, and exploring cooperation. It is an example of functional or issue-specific diplomacy operating alongside an unresolved territorial and maritime dispute.

An ISEAS analysis of the eighth BCM meeting in January 2024 found that the meeting did not produce agreement on concrete cooperative projects, particularly on oil and gas development. The analysis also documented the widening differences between the parties. The Chinese readout raised issues beyond the maritime dispute, including Taiwan, while the Philippine readout did not include that discussion. This suggests that a mechanism established for practical dialogue may become affected by broader strategic disagreements.

The ninth BCM meeting was held in Manila on 2 July 2024. The parties discussed the situation at Second Thomas Shoal, maritime communication, coast guard dialogue, marine science and technology, and environmental protection. On 19 July 2024, the Philippine government announced an understanding on principles and approaches for routine Philippine rotation and resupply missions to BRP Sierra Madre at Ayungin Shoal. The announcement stated that the understanding would be subject to regular review through the BCM and would not prejudice either side's positions.

An AMTI analysis published on 23 March 2026 reported that 13 Philippine rotation and resupply missions had taken place between the provisional arrangement and 4 March 2026. Philippine officials said the missions proceeded without dangerous encounters. The source is useful because it records a concrete operational result: a limited arrangement appears to have reduced immediate friction around a specific activity for a defined period.

However, this should not be generalized into a claim that the maritime dispute had been de-escalated overall. The arrangement was narrow and issue-specific. It did not settle sovereignty, maritime entitlements, or the legal status of the shoal. It was also described by the Philippine side as an understanding of principles and approaches, not a comprehensive agreement resolving the dispute.

Subsequent incidents are an important counterpoint. On 24 September 2026, the Philippine National Task Force for the West Philippine Sea reported that China Coast Guard and People's Liberation Army Navy vessels interfered with a Philippine rotation and resupply mission to Ayungin Shoal. The statement is an official Philippine account and should be treated as such. Other Philippine Coast Guard and government reports in September described additional incidents, including a reported collision involving a Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources vessel and the discharge of flares near an unarmed Philippine Coast Guard aircraft. These reports should be triangulated with Chinese statements and independent reporting before being used as settled descriptions of contested events.

The case therefore presents a more precise conclusion: a narrow operational understanding can produce temporary, observable cooperation in a particular activity, but its durability remains vulnerable to wider strategic tensions and competing interpretations of the parties' rights.

## 6. The 2016 South China Sea Arbitral Award and the Legal Context

The South China Sea Arbitration between the Philippines and China was initiated by the Philippines under Annex VII of UNCLOS. The tribunal issued its award on 12 July 2016. The Permanent Court of Arbitration administered the proceedings; the tribunal itself was constituted under UNCLOS procedures.

The scope of the award must be described accurately. The tribunal expressly stated that it did not decide which state had sovereignty over land territory in the South China Sea. It also did not delimit a maritime boundary between the parties. Instead, it addressed questions concerning the interpretation and application of UNCLOS, including the source of maritime entitlements, the status of maritime features, historic rights, and the legality of certain conduct.

Among its principal conclusions, the tribunal found that historic rights claims within the nine-dash line were incompatible with UNCLOS to the extent that they exceeded the maritime entitlements allowed by the Convention. It found that none of the high-tide features in the Spratly Islands qualified as islands capable of generating an exclusive economic zone or continental shelf under Article 121(3). It also found that Mischief Reef and Second Thomas Shoal were low-tide elevations and that the Philippines held sovereign rights over relevant resources in its exclusive economic zone and continental shelf in those areas, subject to the precise legal findings in the award.

The tribunal also addressed fishing and environmental issues. It found that China had violated the traditional fishing rights of Filipino fishermen at Scarborough Shoal. It found that China's large-scale land reclamation and artificial-island construction caused severe harm to coral reef ecosystems and breached environmental obligations under UNCLOS. It also examined destructive fishing practices and China's duty to protect and preserve the marine environment.

China's official position is that the award is null and void, has no binding force, and is neither accepted nor recognized by China. China argues that the tribunal lacked jurisdiction and that the disputes should be resolved through bilateral negotiation. This rejection is a material part of the political context, but it does not change the text of the award or the tribunal's legal conclusions. The manuscript should explain the distinction between the legal status asserted by the tribunal under UNCLOS and China's refusal to accept the award.

For Chinese Functionalism, the legal context is significant because it shows that functional cooperation does not occur in an institutional vacuum. Maritime cooperation is shaped by legal entitlements, contested jurisdiction, and competing interpretations of international law. An analytical framework that ignores these legal structures would be incomplete.

## 7. Fisheries, Marine Environmental Protection, and Scientific Cooperation

Fisheries and marine environmental protection provide some of the clearest examples of common functional needs in the South China Sea. Fish stocks, coral reefs, endangered species, and marine habitats are shared ecological concerns. Their management requires data, monitoring, conservation rules, enforcement, and cooperation among neighboring authorities.

The 2016 arbitral award provides important evidence of environmental harm and the legal responsibilities of states. It documents the tribunal's findings on large-scale coral reef destruction associated with island-building and on destructive harvesting practices involving giant clams and other endangered species. These findings make marine environmental protection more than a general area of potential cooperation. They identify concrete ecological problems that require sustained governance.

ASEAN and China have repeatedly identified marine environmental protection and scientific research as areas for practical cooperation. The DOC Work Plan and subsequent official meeting readouts support the existence of an institutional agenda in these fields. However, the public sources reviewed for this dossier do not establish a comprehensive record of joint fisheries management, shared stock assessments, coordinated enforcement, or independently evaluated restoration outcomes.

This gap is itself relevant. It is possible to have diplomatic agreement on the desirability of marine science and environmental cooperation while failing to establish effective joint management in the most contested areas. The next research step should identify specific projects, their locations, participating agencies, shared data arrangements, scientific publications, monitoring results, and measurable ecological outcomes.

The manuscript should also distinguish conservation cooperation from law-enforcement cooperation. A state may support marine environmental workshops but still contest another state's fishing access or enforcement authority. Functional cooperation in one domain does not automatically remove conflict in another.

## 8. Differentiated Assertiveness and Variation Between Claimants

Joseph Ching Velasco's 2026 open-access article, already registered as SRC-0023, proposes the concept of differentiated assertiveness to explain variation in China's maritime behavior toward the Philippines and Vietnam. The article argues that China's use of coercion and restraint is shaped by its perception of the other state's external alignment and domestic political characteristics. It compares maritime incidents involving China and the Philippines with those involving China and Vietnam during 2020 to 2025.

This contribution is relevant to the Chinese Functionalism project for two reasons. First, it warns against treating China's maritime behavior as uniform across all Southeast Asian states. Second, it provides a competing framework focused on the calibration of coercion rather than on the organization of functional cooperation.

The claim should be treated as an author's explanatory argument, not an uncontested finding. The manuscript should examine the study's incident selection, coding rules, definitions of coercion and restraint, and treatment of other possible explanations such as geography, operational tempo, claimant capabilities, and the salience of particular features. Its findings may be useful for comparative case selection, but the project should not simply adopt its explanation without review.

The comparison also reinforces the need to disaggregate smaller-state agency. Vietnam's maritime strategy, alliance posture, diplomatic practice, and domestic political system differ from those of the Philippines. A framework that treats ASEAN states as interchangeable will miss important variation.

## 9. Theoretical Implications

### 9.1 Classical functionalism

The maritime case demonstrates the continuing relevance of functional needs. Search and rescue, navigation safety, marine pollution, fisheries, and scientific research create reasons for cooperation even where political disputes remain unresolved. The DOC explicitly recognizes this possibility. However, state authority remains central to defining the scope and terms of cooperation. The case therefore challenges any simple assumption that technical agencies can be insulated from political conflict.

### 9.2 Neofunctionalism

There is limited evidence of automatic spillover from practical maritime cooperation to broader political integration. Repeated meetings and workshops have not removed disagreements over maritime entitlements or produced a concluded COC. The provisional arrangement at Second Thomas Shoal shows that a narrow operational understanding may work for a particular task without transforming the broader relationship. Spillover is possible as a hypothesis, but it must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### 9.3 Liberal institutionalism and liberal intergovernmentalism

The DOC, BCM, SOM-DOC, JWG-DOC, and COC negotiations show the value of recurring institutional channels. These forums provide information, repeated contact, procedures, and opportunities to manage incidents. At the same time, progress depends on the preferences and bargaining positions of sovereign governments. Liberal intergovernmentalism is therefore a strong alternative explanation for the slow and negotiated development of regional maritime arrangements.

### 9.4 Economic statecraft and security studies

The maritime domain is not primarily an economic case, but it reveals the limits of reducing functional cooperation to technical management. Coast guard operations, maritime militia, naval presence, and enforcement encounters can be instruments of state strategy. Economic statecraft does not explain every maritime incident, but strategic coercion, deterrence, and security competition are essential competing perspectives.

### 9.5 Complex interdependence

The South China Sea contains multiple channels of interaction among governments, coast guards, fisheries agencies, shipping interests, scientific institutions, and regional forums. These connections create opportunities for practical cooperation. They also create points of vulnerability. Interdependence does not necessarily reduce the salience of force when territorial claims and sovereign rights are at stake.

### 9.6 The proposed Chinese Functionalism framework

The maritime evidence may help develop the project's analytical checklist, but it does not establish a distinct theory. It suggests that the analysis should ask:

1. What functional need motivates cooperation?
2. What institutional form organizes it, and how much authority does that institution possess?
3. What is actually implemented, and what outcomes can be observed?
4. How are benefits, costs, access, and risks distributed?
5. How does power asymmetry shape the terms and enforcement of cooperation?
6. How do smaller states negotiate, resist, adapt, or use external institutions and legal mechanisms?

A further cross-cutting question is whether cooperation spills into other domains, remains compartmentalized, or is interrupted by conflict. This should be examined as an empirical outcome rather than treated as a built-in mechanism.

## 10. Provisional Propositions for Further Testing

The following propositions are research hypotheses, not established findings:

**Proposition 1:** Practical maritime cooperation can proceed in narrowly defined domains even when sovereignty and jurisdictional disputes remain unresolved, particularly when activities are limited in scope and do not prejudice the parties' stated positions.

**Proposition 2:** Issue-specific arrangements may reduce immediate operational risk without producing a broader political settlement. Their effectiveness should therefore be evaluated at the level of the particular activity they regulate.

**Proposition 3:** Institutionalized dialogue can persist despite limited substantive agreement. Repeated meetings should not be treated as evidence of successful dispute resolution unless they produce observable changes in behavior or outcomes.

**Proposition 4:** Power asymmetry shapes the ability of states to define access, operational procedures, and enforcement practices. Smaller-state agency may still operate through diplomacy, law, coalition-building, public communication, and selective cooperation.

**Proposition 5:** Functional cooperation is more likely to remain compartmentalized when the issue is closely connected to sovereignty, military access, or strategic control. Whether cooperation spills over into wider political accommodation depends on additional conditions that require empirical investigation.

## 11. Recommended Case-Study Design

The maritime chapter should not attempt to cover every South China Sea dispute equally. It should use a small number of analytically contrasting cases:

1. **ASEAN-China DOC and COC process:** institutionalized regional dialogue, practical cooperation priorities, and prolonged negotiation.
2. **Philippines-China BCM:** bilateral consultation, limited practical cooperation, and recurring disagreements.
3. **Second Thomas Shoal provisional arrangement:** a narrow operational accommodation with evidence of a temporary period of reduced friction followed by renewed reports of interference.
4. **Philippines-Vietnam comparison:** variation in maritime interactions and the limits of generalizing from one claimant state to another.
5. **Marine environmental protection:** a test of whether shared ecological interests produce sustained scientific and management cooperation in contested waters.

Each case should distinguish between stated objectives, institutional arrangements, implementation, outputs, outcomes, and wider political effects.

## 12. Evidence Gaps and Cautions

The current evidence base has several limits:

- Official statements document the positions of the issuing government or institution. They do not independently verify every event or claim.
- The text of the July 2024 provisional arrangement has not been published in full in the sources reviewed here. Its precise obligations and verification mechanisms therefore remain unclear.
- The AMTI count of 13 resupply missions relies in part on Philippine official accounts. It supports analysis of the reported operational pattern but should not be treated as a comprehensive independent incident database.
- Reports of September 2026 incidents are based on Philippine government or Coast Guard statements. Chinese responses and independent corroboration should be sought before drawing broader conclusions.
- ASEAN and China report recurring workshops and consultations, but public evidence of project-level outputs and ecological outcomes remains limited.
- The COC remained under negotiation as of 1 November 2026. Any later development should be checked before publication.
- The 2016 award did not resolve land sovereignty or maritime boundary delimitation. It should not be described as doing so.
- The Velasco 2026 article offers a competing explanation for differentiated Chinese behavior. Its methodology and incident coding require full review.

## 13. Implications for the Manuscript

The maritime material should be integrated into the manuscript as a test of the proposed framework's limits, not as proof that functional cooperation has pacified the region. The most defensible argument is that functional cooperation and strategic conflict can coexist, sometimes within the same bilateral relationship and even around the same maritime feature.

The DOC and the COC process show the institutionalization of practical cooperation and diplomatic negotiation. The BCM and the July 2024 provisional arrangement show that narrow operational accommodations can be negotiated under conditions of serious disagreement. The 2016 arbitral award and subsequent maritime incidents demonstrate that legal rules, power asymmetry, and conflicting interests remain consequential. Together, these cases allow the book to examine the conditions under which practical cooperation is possible, the limited outcomes it can produce, and the reasons it may fail to transform the wider relationship.

The maritime evidence does not yet show that Chinese Functionalism explains these patterns better than institutionalism, intergovernmental bargaining, international law, or security studies. Its immediate value is as a structured analytical lens that forces the research to examine functional need, institutional form, actual outcomes, distribution, asymmetry, and smaller-state agency together. Whether that combination amounts to a distinctive explanatory contribution remains an open question for the book.

## Recommended Integration into the Book

This dossier is best integrated into:

- Part II: China and Functional Cooperation, as a dedicated discussion of maritime cooperation and its limits.
- Part III: Developing Chinese Functionalism, as a negative and boundary-testing case for spillover, institutional binding, and smaller-state agency.
- Part IV: The Philippine Perspective, especially the 2016 arbitration, BCM, provisional resupply arrangement, and subsequent reported incidents.

The maritime chapter should be framed around the coexistence of practical cooperation and strategic contestation. It should not imply that cooperation is absent, nor that dialogue has resolved the dispute. Its value is to show the conditions under which limited cooperation can persist, the constraints imposed by power asymmetry and legal disagreement, and the evidence required to assess whether functional cooperation changes political relations.
