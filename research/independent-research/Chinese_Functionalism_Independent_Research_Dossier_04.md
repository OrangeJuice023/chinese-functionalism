# Independent Research Dossier 04

## Chinese IR Theory, Functionalism, and the Question of Conceptual Originality

**Project:** Chinese Functionalism: Sectoral Cooperation, Power Asymmetry, and Regional Order in Southeast Asia  
**Prepared:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** Independent research memorandum for integration and human review  
**Scope:** Exploratory English- and Chinese-language open-web literature search. This is not a systematic review of CNKI, Wanfang, CSSCI, university library holdings, or all Chinese-language publications.

---

## Executive summary

This dossier addresses a question that sits at the centre of the book: is “Chinese Functionalism” an identifiable concept in Chinese international relations (IR) scholarship, an official or semi-official doctrine of Chinese foreign policy, or a new analytical label proposed by this project?

The preliminary search does **not** establish Chinese Functionalism as a recognized, coherent school of IR theory. Research on the “Chinese School” commonly discusses relational theory, moral realism, Tianxia, and symbiosis theory. The Chinese School itself remains debated. Some scholars describe several distinct Chinese theoretical contributions rather than one unified school, while other scholars argue that the school exists in plural forms. Critics also question the relationship between claims of intellectual originality, Chinese traditions, and the continued influence of Western theoretical categories.

This does not mean that functionalism is absent from Chinese IR scholarship. The term appears as an established analytical approach used to interpret international organizations, regional integration, and South-South cooperation. Recent Chinese-language research applies other frameworks, including institutional-nesting theory and international practice theory, to China-ASEAN maritime cooperation and wider South-South cooperation. These works are directly relevant to the project, but they do not establish a distinct theory called Chinese Functionalism.

The most defensible position remains to treat **Chinese Functionalism as a project-proposed analytical lens**, not as an existing Chinese school or official Chinese doctrine. It may be used to investigate how sectoral and institutional cooperation operates under asymmetric conditions, but the project must demonstrate what this lens adds beyond functionalism, intergovernmentalism, economic statecraft, complex interdependence, global value-chain analysis, and regional institutionalism.

A further implication follows. The word “Chinese” in the title needs explicit justification. It may refer to the empirical focus on China’s practices, rather than to a theory that originated in Chinese intellectual traditions. The book should make that distinction visible to readers and invite collaborators to decide whether the working title communicates the project accurately.

## 1. Scope and method of this search

The search examined English-language publications and accessible Chinese-language academic pages and abstracts. It included searches for the exact English phrase “Chinese Functionalism,” Chinese-language combinations of “功能主义” (functionalism), “中国” (China), “国际关系” (international relations), and “中国学派” (Chinese School), and literature on Chinese IR theory and China-ASEAN cooperation.

The search found:

1. A substantial debate about the existence, composition, and intellectual contribution of a Chinese School of IR.
2. Functionalism discussed as a general IR approach or as one explanation of cooperation, rather than as a distinct Chinese school.
3. Recent Chinese-language studies of China-ASEAN maritime cooperation that use institutional-nesting and cooperation-framework approaches, including findings that technical cooperation has not necessarily become durable institutional output.
4. A 2026 Chinese-language article on South-South cooperation that reviews functionalist cooperation as one existing explanatory perspective and proposes international practice theory as an alternative lens.

The search did not identify a well-established Chinese IR theory formally named “Chinese Functionalism.” This is a **limited search result, not proof of non-existence**. A defensible claim about the term’s absence would require systematic searches of CNKI, Wanfang, CSSCI journals, university dissertations, Chinese-language books, and relevant policy publications. These databases may include records not indexed by general web search.

## 2. What the Chinese School debate actually contains

### 2.1 The Chinese School is contested, not a single settled theory

Peng Lu’s 2024 review describes the Chinese School debate as having moved from efforts at theoretical innovation toward the further development of several visible approaches, particularly relational theory, moral realism, and symbiosis theory. The article’s title itself signals the contested character of the project: “ignored process,” “controversial progress,” and “uncertain prospects.” It is therefore misleading to treat the Chinese School as a single unified theory with an agreed ontology or explanatory mechanism.

Peter J. Katzenstein’s 2024 article, “Is There a Chinese School of IR Theory?”, offers an explicitly plural answer. Its abstract argues that a Chinese School does not exist in the singular, but does exist in the plural. This is useful for the present project because it cautions against making “Chinese” a synonym for one coherent intellectual tradition.

Ren Xiao’s 2020 Chinese-language discussion of the Chinese School similarly presents the concept as a collection of theoretical works produced by scholars primarily working in China. He identifies relational theory, moral realism, Tianxia theory, and symbiosis theory as important contributions, while acknowledging disagreement about the label itself. The underlying point is that the Chinese School is better approached as a developing and internally diverse field of theory production than as a unified doctrine.

**Implication for this project:** Chinese Functionalism should not be presented as the next established branch of the Chinese School unless research demonstrates that Chinese IR scholars have actually articulated it as such. The project can propose a new analytical concept, but it must identify that move as its own intellectual contribution and submit it to comparison and criticism.

### 2.2 The main theoretical approaches are not simply functionalist variants

The literature identifies several influential approaches:

- **Relational theory:** associated with Qin Yaqing, it treats actors as constituted through relations and gives analytical priority to processes and relationships in motion. It is not simply a theory of sectoral cooperation or institutional spillover.
- **Moral realism:** associated with Yan Xuetong, it brings moral leadership and political authority into realist analysis. Its central concerns differ from functional agencies and task-specific cooperation.
- **Tianxia:** associated with Zhao Tingyang, it explores a world-ordering conception rooted in Chinese political philosophy. It addresses inclusion, order, and the political imagination of world governance.
- **Symbiosis theory:** associated with Ren Xiao and other scholars, it emphasizes coexistence, mutual dependence, and the possibility of development amid difference. It is not interchangeable with Mitrany’s functionalism.

Ren Xiao’s 2024 article on non-Western IR theory and the 2024 Chinese Journal of International Politics forum on Chinese School(s) both situate these approaches within broader debates about theoretical pluralism and the contribution of non-Western scholarship. The forum brings Chinese theorists into dialogue with scholars working in other traditions. This is important because the book should not assume that a Chinese-origin concept is analytically distinct merely because it uses Chinese historical or philosophical references.

### 2.3 The debate also concerns knowledge production and power

Yih-Jye Hwang’s 2021 postcolonial reassessment asks how the Chinese School should be understood in relation to power, resistance, and the dominant structures of IR knowledge production. His analysis complicates any simple claim that Chinese theories are either wholly independent of Western approaches or merely imitations of them.

Miaofang Guan and Feliciano de Sá Guimarães’s review of the construction of IR theory in China traces the field’s development and identifies several drivers: dissatisfaction with the dominance of Western models, renewed engagement with classical Chinese thought, and China’s changing global position. The publication metadata should be checked carefully because repository and journal records show a publication-year discrepancy. The DOI and volume details should be confirmed before final citation.

**Implication:** If the book wishes to claim that its proposed framework is “Chinese” in an intellectual or civilizational sense, it must explain which intellectual resources are being used and how they shape the framework’s concepts or mechanisms. If its focus is instead China’s foreign-policy practice, the title should say so clearly enough to avoid implying that the framework is an established Chinese School theory.

## 3. How functionalism appears in Chinese-language IR discussion

The preliminary search indicates that functionalism is used in Chinese-language IR scholarship as a general theoretical perspective. It is not absent from the literature. However, its use does not in itself establish a distinct “Chinese Functionalism.”

A 2026 article by Li Zongfang and Wang Dong, “International Practice Theory Perspective on South-South Cooperation,” published in *International Forum*, reviews several explanations of South-South cooperation. The accessible journal page’s AI-generated summary lists power competition, functionalist cooperation, and constructivism among existing perspectives. It characterizes functionalist cooperation as an account in which rational states cooperate to reduce transaction costs and share development experience, then argues that practice theory can offer a more dynamic account of how cooperation evolves through background knowledge and recurring practices.

A caution is necessary: the accessible page labels this material as an AI summary, not the article’s original abstract. Its description is therefore a research lead, not a substitute for reading the article itself. The article’s metadata is visible, but its complete argument and evidence require full-text review.

A Peking University-hosted discussion of international organization research also describes functionalism as distinguishing functional problems from political and security issues. This indicates that functionalism continues to appear as a recognized theoretical category in Chinese discussions of international organization. It does not establish a Chinese-origin functionalist school.

**Implication:** The project must distinguish three separate propositions:

1. Chinese scholars use or discuss functionalism as an existing IR approach.
2. China and its partners engage in functional cooperation across sectors.
3. A distinct theory or doctrine called Chinese Functionalism exists.

The first two propositions have some support in the sources located. The third remains unproven.

## 4. Recent Chinese-language studies of China-ASEAN cooperation

### 4.1 Institutional nesting and maritime cooperation

Jiang Xiumin and Zheng Xin’s 2025 article, “Beyond Zero-Sum Game: Reconstructing the China-ASEAN Maritime Cooperation System from the Perspective of Institutional Nesting Theory,” is especially relevant. The abstract identifies three tensions in China-ASEAN maritime cooperation:

- Fragmented institutional provision has slowed negotiations on the South China Sea Code of Conduct and the operation of the maritime cooperation fund.
- Differences in values and political understandings contribute to disagreement between claims of full sovereignty and proposals for joint development.
- Technical cooperation pilots have not yet been effectively translated into institutional outcomes.

The authors propose a more nested governance structure connecting hard and soft law, shared understandings, and technical tools. The project should not adopt their prescriptions uncritically. Their diagnosis is more important for our purposes: technical cooperation can precede, but does not automatically produce, institutional consolidation.

This is a direct challenge to any simple spillover assumption. It also offers a Chinese-language scholarly perspective that can be compared with the project’s maritime dossier.

### 4.2 The Code of Conduct and the China-ASEAN cooperation framework

Zheng Zhihao’s 2025 article on textual disagreements in the South China Sea Code of Conduct negotiations examines disagreements over geographic scope, legal binding force, dispute-settlement mechanisms, and restraint. The abstract presents the COC as a China-ASEAN rule-making effort and argues that the wider cooperation framework may become more regularized and institutionalized through dialogue, cooperation, and dispute-resolution mechanisms.

This article is relevant because it shows that institutional design is politically contested. The parties do not merely need technical solutions; they disagree over the jurisdiction, legal force, and procedures that would govern those solutions. This reinforces the need to analyze the content of institutions, not merely count meetings, declarations, or cooperation mechanisms.

### 4.3 Why these sources matter

Both studies use analytical frameworks other than a distinct Chinese Functionalism. Yet both examine problems central to this book: whether technical cooperation becomes institutionalized, how sovereignty disputes constrain cooperation, and how China-ASEAN mechanisms evolve under political disagreement.

They should be added to the literature review as Chinese-language scholarship engaging the same empirical and conceptual problem. They should not be misclassified as evidence that Chinese Functionalism already exists.

## 5. Four possible meanings of “Chinese Functionalism”

The project should decide which of the following meanings it intends to use. They are not interchangeable.

### Meaning A: An official Chinese doctrine

This would mean that Chinese leaders, institutions, or policy documents explicitly advance a functionalist doctrine for international relations. The current research does not establish this. Official references to cooperation, development, connectivity, or common interests are not sufficient proof of a functionalist doctrine.

### Meaning B: A distinct school within Chinese IR theory

This would mean that Chinese IR scholars have developed a coherent functionalist theory grounded in Chinese intellectual traditions. The sources reviewed do not establish this either. Current discussion of Chinese IR theory foregrounds several other approaches and remains internally contested.

### Meaning C: A project-proposed analytical adaptation

This would mean that the authors propose a lens for examining China’s sectoral and institutional cooperation under conditions of power asymmetry. This is the most defensible current formulation. It does not claim that the concept is already accepted in Chinese scholarship or official discourse. It does, however, carry a burden of proof: it must show what it identifies or explains beyond existing theories.

### Meaning D: A descriptive study of China’s functional cooperation

This would abandon the claim that the project is proposing a new theory. The book would instead examine China’s functional cooperation with Southeast Asia, its institutional forms, distributional effects, and political limits. This may be the strongest option if the comparative analysis shows that the proposed framework adds little explanatory value.

## 6. What would make the proposed framework analytically distinct?

The framework cannot become distinct simply by combining familiar concepts such as sectoral cooperation, interdependence, power asymmetry, and smaller-state agency. Those concepts already appear across functionalism, economic statecraft, interdependence theory, institutionalism, and regionalism research.

The project needs to specify a mechanism and demonstrate that it captures a pattern those theories leave insufficiently explained. At minimum, it should answer the following questions.

### 6.1 Unit of analysis

Is the framework examining individual cooperation projects, sectoral networks, institutions, bilateral relationships, or regional systems? Without a defined unit, the framework risks shifting between a railway project, an ASEAN-wide trade regime, and a maritime dispute as if they were comparable observations.

### 6.2 Causal or analytical mechanism

A possible sequence for testing is:

**Functional need → negotiated sectoral arrangement → implementation and distribution of benefits → changes in dependence and institutional practice → political effects, resistance, or compartmentalization.**

Each arrow requires evidence. Cooperation may not create greater interdependence. Interdependence may not create political alignment. Institutional practice may remain confined to a narrow sector. The mechanism must allow for failure, reversal, and non-spillover.

### 6.3 Conditional factors

The project should test whether outcomes vary with:

- The asymmetry of economic, financial, technological, or military capabilities.
- The degree of control retained by national governments.
- The institutional design and legal enforceability of arrangements.
- Domestic state capacity and regulatory institutions.
- The intensity of unresolved territorial or security disputes.
- The ability of smaller states to diversify partners, renegotiate terms, or reject proposals.

These are possible conditioning variables, not established findings.

### 6.4 Comparative test

To justify the word “Chinese” as more than an empirical label, the project should compare China’s practices with at least one other major external partner or regional cooperation model. Japan’s infrastructure and development cooperation in Southeast Asia, US digital and security initiatives, EU regulatory engagement, or India’s development partnerships may offer useful comparisons. The comparison should be tightly scoped and not become a separate book.

If the same mechanisms and outcomes appear across all cases, the project may have identified a general framework for functional cooperation under asymmetry rather than a specifically Chinese one.

## 7. Recommended positioning for the book

The most defensible current wording is:

> This book develops and tests “Chinese Functionalism” as a provisional analytical lens for examining China’s sectoral and institutional cooperation with Southeast Asian states under conditions of power asymmetry. The term does not refer to an established Chinese IR school or an officially declared Chinese foreign-policy doctrine. Its analytical value depends on whether it explains patterns of cooperation, institutional development, distributional effects, and smaller-state responses that existing theories do not adequately capture.

This definition does three things. It makes clear that the term is proposed by the project. It identifies the empirical and analytical focus. It states the standard by which the concept must be judged.

The title may still be retained during collaborator review, but the subtitle and introduction should prevent readers from assuming that “Chinese Functionalism” is an established school. A more conservative alternative is *China’s Functional Cooperation in Southeast Asia: Power Asymmetry, Institutions, and Regional Order*.

The next discussion with Professor Carlos and Sir Bam should include the following questions:

1. Should “Chinese Functionalism” remain the working title if the concept is a project-proposed analytical lens rather than an established Chinese School theory?
2. Does the project seek to explain Chinese foreign-policy practice, develop a new analytical framework, or do both?
3. What would count as sufficient evidence that the framework adds value beyond economic statecraft and intergovernmentalism?
4. Should Chinese-language IR scholarship become a dedicated chapter or a substantial section within the theoretical foundations?
5. Which comparison would best test whether the proposed pattern is specifically Chinese or more generally associated with major-power cooperation under asymmetry?
6. Should the project retain a theory-building ambition, or adopt a more conservative comparative study of China’s functional cooperation?

## 10. Conclusion

The research located so far supports a careful but important conclusion. Functionalism is present in Chinese-language IR discussions as an established analytical approach, and Chinese scholars are actively studying China-ASEAN cooperation through other theoretical frameworks. However, the material reviewed does not establish “Chinese Functionalism” as a recognized school, doctrine, or coherent theory.

This is not a reason to abandon the project. It is a reason to define its intellectual contribution more precisely. The book may still develop a useful analytical lens, but it must not imply that the concept already exists as an accepted Chinese theory. Its contribution will depend on the quality of its comparative tests, its treatment of negative evidence, and its ability to explain something that existing frameworks do not explain as well.

## Source-use limitations

This dossier draws on publication metadata, accessible abstracts, institutional repository pages, and selected Chinese-language article pages. It does not claim full-text review of every item. The 2026 South-South cooperation page includes an AI-generated summary; it must not be cited as if it were the authors’ own abstract. The Chinese-language search was exploratory and cannot establish that no publication has ever used the exact phrase “Chinese Functionalism.” Full-text review and systematic database searching remain necessary before final claims are made.
