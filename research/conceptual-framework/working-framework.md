# Working Framework: Chinese Functionalism (Provisional)

[PROVISIONAL — REQUIRES HUMAN REVIEW]

## Theoretical Assumptions
1. States pursue cooperation in functional/sectoral domains where mutual gains are identifiable.
2. Functional cooperation can generate interdependence and institutional habits.
3. Power asymmetry shapes who benefits and who bears costs.
4. Cooperation may serve strategic as well as welfare objectives.

## Proposed Mechanisms
- Sectoral cooperation (trade, infrastructure, technology, health).
- Institutional engagement (ASEAN+1, RCEP, ACFTA, BRI).
- Interdependence creation.
- Norm/standard diffusion.
- Capacity-building and development finance.

## Observable Indicators
- Scope and depth of sectoral agreements.
- Institutional participation and leadership roles.
- Trade/investment flows and infrastructure projects.
- Frequency and coverage of functional cooperation mechanisms.
- Smaller-state policy responses (hedging, bandwagoning, resistance).

## Scope Conditions
- Applies where functional cooperation is present and measurable.
- May be strongest in economic/technical domains; weaker in security domains.

## Limitations
- Risk of conflating pragmatic statecraft with functionalist doctrine.
- Difficulty distinguishing mutual benefit from strategic leverage.
- Western theoretical concepts may not capture Chinese practice.
- Power asymmetry may undermine functionalist optimism.

## Falsifying Evidence
- If functional cooperation consistently fails to produce interdependence or institutional binding.
- If cooperation is purely extractive with no mutual gains.
- If smaller states systematically resist or exit cooperation.

## Distinction from Existing Theories
- Unlike Mitrany's functionalism, does not assume de-politicization or anti-statism.
- Unlike neofunctionalism, does not predict automatic political integration.
- Unlike liberal institutionalism, centers power asymmetry and strategic interests.
- Unlike Chinese IR theories, is practice-grounded and sectoral rather than civilizational.
