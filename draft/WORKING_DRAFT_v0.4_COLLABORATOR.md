## Part I: Understanding Functionalism

### 1. The Problem of International Cooperation

International cooperation is one of the most studied subjects in international relations, yet it remains conceptually unsettled. States cooperate in trade, security, environment, health, and technology, but the reasons, mechanisms, and consequences of cooperation vary enormously across cases. Existing theories offer valuable insights, but they also carry assumptions that may not hold when power is distributed unevenly.

This manuscript examines one particular form of cooperation, namely functional cooperation, and asks whether it can help explain China's international engagement. Functional cooperation refers to collaboration in specific sectors or technical domains rather than comprehensive political integration. It includes trade agreements, infrastructure projects, technology standards, health initiatives, and institutional arrangements that address concrete problems without requiring full political union.

The distinction between cooperation as an observable practice and functionalism as an analytical approach is crucial. States cooperate for many reasons: mutual benefit, institutional habit, strategic calculation, or coercion. Functionalism, by contrast, is a theoretical tradition that explains how sectoral cooperation can generate broader political and social integration. The mere existence of functional cooperation between China and other states does not demonstrate that a functionalist dynamic is at work.

The concept of Chinese Functionalism is proposed as a possible analytical framework, not as an established theory. It asks whether China's use of sectoral and institutional cooperation under conditions of power asymmetry can be understood through a functionalist lens, and whether doing so adds analytical value beyond existing theories. The project remains open to the possibility that established theories explain the evidence more convincingly.

### 2. Classical Functionalism and David Mitrany

David Mitrany is commonly regarded as the originator of the functional approach to international organization. Writing during the interwar period and the Second World War, Mitrany proposed that international peace could be advanced through function specific agencies addressing concrete human needs rather than through comprehensive political federation (Mitrany, 1943; Mitrany, 1948).

Mitrany's central argument was that the functional approach emphasizes the common index of need rather than the individual index of power (Mitrany, 1948). He believed that many needs cut across national boundaries and that joint government could be provided for them without requiring full political union. Functional arrangements are complementary rather than mutually exclusive, allowing varied membership and autonomous development. The nature of each function determines the scope and powers required for its effective performance, a principle Mitrany called technical self-determination (Mitrany, 1948).

Mitrany's approach was also anti statist in orientation. He regarded nationalism as a primary cause of political evils and believed that international organizations should undermine the state as the sole form of social organization by diffusing political power across functional agencies. However, later scholarship has cautioned against treating Mitrany as a founding father in a simple sense. His work drew on earlier technocratic and socialist traditions, including the ideas of Leonard Woolf and G. D. H. Cole, and he functioned more as a catalyst who combined pragmatic and utopian elements into a new synthesis (Mitrany and the Emergence of the Functional Approach, 2017).

The classical functionalist framework carries significant limitations. It assumes that technical tasks can be separated from politics, an assumption that subsequent scholarship has challenged. It tends to underestimate power asymmetry and the strategic interests of states. It also offers limited guidance for situations where functional cooperation intersects with territorial disputes or security competition.

### 3. Neofunctionalism and Regional Integration

Ernst B. Haas developed neofunctionalism as a modification of classical functionalism, applying it to regional integration. In *The Uniting of Europe* (1958), Haas argued that integration proceeds through spillover, in which cooperation in one sector generates pressures for cooperation in related sectors, ultimately leading to political integration (Haas, 1958).

Neofunctionalism identifies several mechanisms. Technical or functional spillover occurs when cooperation in one area creates functional demands for cooperation in adjacent areas. Political spillover occurs when supranational officials act as entrepreneurs, expanding the scope of integration. Cultivated spillover involves the mobilization of interest groups and citizens around regional solutions. Haas also acknowledged spillback, the reversal of integration (Haas, 1964).

The neofunctionalist thesis was ambitious. It predicted that economic interdependence and functional pressures would lead, through incremental steps, to a supranational political community. Critics argued that this prediction was not borne out by events, particularly the stagnation of European integration in the 1960s and 1970s. Moravcsik contended that neofunctionalism was over ambitious, one sided, and essentially unfalsifiable, because it sought to explain long term change without micro foundational theories of preferences, bargaining, and institutional delegation (Moravcsik, 2005, as cited in the neofunctionalism literature).

Haas himself contributed to the decline of interest in his own theory by declaring neofunctionalism obsolescent in the early 1970s. Nevertheless, later scholarship has argued that the neofunctionalist conceptual repertoire, including spillover and task expansion, remains analytically productive for understanding European and comparative regional integration (Ruggie, 2005).

### 4. Critiques and Theoretical Alternatives

Functionalism and neofunctionalism must be distinguished from several related approaches. Liberal institutionalism emphasizes how institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate cooperation under anarchy, but it tends to assume shared interests and pays less attention to power asymmetry. Liberal intergovernmentalism, associated with Moravcsik, explains integration through state preferences, interstate bargaining, and institutional delegation. This approach may better explain China-ASEAN cooperation, which appears driven by state interests and bargaining rather than automatic spillover.

Economic statecraft focuses on the use of economic instruments for strategic objectives, including incentives, coercion, and leverage. It overlaps with functional cooperation but centers power and intentionality more explicitly. Complex interdependence highlights multiple channels of contact and the reduced role of military force, yet it can understate coercion and security competition. Constructivism focuses on norms and identities, which may underweight material power.

These distinctions matter for the Chinese Functionalism project. If the proposed framework merely restates insights from liberal institutionalism, economic statecraft, or complex interdependence, it adds little. Its potential contribution lies in centering power asymmetry, strategic interests, and the experience of smaller states within a functionalist vocabulary, while remaining open to the possibility that established theories explain the evidence more convincingly.

## Part II: China and Functional Cooperation

### 5. China's Approach to International and Regional Cooperation

China's international engagement has expanded significantly over the past two decades. It participates in global and regional institutions, promotes trade and investment, advances infrastructure connectivity, and pursues sectoral cooperation across multiple domains. These developments are well documented, yet their theoretical meaning remains contested.

Chinese international relations scholarship has developed several indigenous theoretical frameworks, including moral realism, relational theory, Tianxia, and social symbiosis. These frameworks draw on Chinese philosophical traditions and address questions of order, morality, and relationality. None is explicitly functionalist in the Mitrany or Haas sense (He, 2015). The broader debate on constructing international relations theory with Chinese characteristics has both academic and policy dimensions, and the two should not be conflated (He, 2015).

### 6. China-ASEAN Functional Cooperation

China's engagement with ASEAN provides the most developed empirical context for examining functional cooperation. The relationship has evolved from dialogue partnership to comprehensive strategic partnership, with cooperation spanning trade, investment, infrastructure, digital connectivity, energy, health, education, and maritime affairs (ASEAN Secretariat, 2024; ASEAN Secretariat, 2025).

Trade and investment constitute the most quantifiable dimension. In 2025, two way merchandise trade between ASEAN and China reached USD 913.1 billion, and Chinese foreign direct investment into ASEAN amounted to USD 26.2 billion (ASEAN Secretariat, 2026). These are preliminary official statistics and should be independently verified. China has been ASEAN's largest trading partner for eighteen consecutive years (ASEAN Secretariat, 2026).

Institutional mechanisms have proliferated. The ASEAN-China Free Trade Area, upgraded to ACFTA 3.0 with commitments in digital economy, green economy, supply chain connectivity, and MSME development, represents a significant institutional achievement (ASEAN Secretariat, 2024). The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership further deepens regional economic integration. The Plan of Action 2026 to 2030 outlines sectoral priorities including trade, connectivity, digital transformation, energy, and people to people exchanges (ASEAN Secretariat, 2025).

### 7. Infrastructure, Trade, Technology, and Institutional Engagement

Infrastructure connectivity illustrates the functional dimension clearly. Projects such as the China-Laos Railway and the Jakarta-Bandung High Speed Railway address concrete connectivity deficits in Southeast Asia. China's comparative advantages in infrastructure construction, combined with ASEAN's capital and technology gaps, create conditions for functional cooperation (Li and Zhang, 2021).

Digital cooperation represents an emerging domain. Initiatives include digital economy partnerships, e-commerce facilitation, and technology standards discussions. However, the gap between policy declarations and measurable outcomes remains significant. Technology standards, data governance, and cybersecurity introduce new dimensions of competition alongside cooperation.

### 8. Cooperation, Competition, and Power Asymmetry

The relationship between functional cooperation and strategic interests is the central analytical problem. Functional cooperation can serve multiple purposes simultaneously: addressing welfare needs, creating interdependence, building institutional habits, and generating political leverage. Distinguishing these motives empirically is difficult.

Critical scholarship has highlighted that China's infrastructure and connectivity initiatives may serve strategic goals, including expanding regional influence. The Belt and Road Initiative has been described as contributing to China's strategic goal of expanding its influence in the region through connectivity and cooperation (Springer, 2024). At the same time, ASEAN members have generally welcomed infrastructure investment while seeking to maintain autonomy and avoid excessive dependence.

This tension between cooperation and asymmetry is not unique to the China-ASEAN relationship. It reflects a broader pattern in which functional cooperation between unequal partners generates both mutual benefits and distributional concerns. Smaller states have developed strategies to navigate this asymmetry, including hedging, which combines engagement with insurance against potential risks (Kuik, 2008). However, hedging does not apply uniformly; state responses vary with capacity, economic structure, and security interests.

## Part III: Developing Chinese Functionalism

### 9. Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Functionalism?

The search for explicitly Chinese functionalist theory in the international relations literature yields limited results. Chinese IR scholarship has developed several indigenous theoretical frameworks, including moral realism, relational theory, Tianxia, and social symbiosis. None is explicitly functionalist in the Mitrany or Haas sense (He, 2015). The Chinese School debate reflects a quest for national identity and global status, with both academic and policy dimensions (He, 2015).

The implication is clear. Chinese Functionalism is not an established academic theory. It is a proposed analytical framework that must be developed and tested. The project must distinguish between the existence of functional cooperation in China's practice, which is well documented, and the existence of a functionalist foreign policy doctrine, which remains an open question.

### 10. The Proposed Analytical Adaptation

The following framework is provisional and subject to revision based on evidence and human review.

Theoretical assumptions: states pursue cooperation in functional domains where mutual gains are identifiable; functional cooperation can generate interdependence and institutional habits; power asymmetry shapes who benefits and who bears costs; cooperation may serve strategic as well as welfare objectives.

Mechanisms: sectoral cooperation in trade, infrastructure, technology, and health; institutional engagement through ASEAN plus one, RCEP, ACFTA, and the Belt and Road Initiative; interdependence creation; norm and standard diffusion; capacity building and development finance.

Observable indicators: scope and depth of sectoral agreements; institutional participation and leadership roles; trade and investment flows; infrastructure project portfolios; frequency and coverage of cooperation mechanisms; smaller state policy responses, including hedging, bandwagoning, and resistance.

Scope conditions: the framework applies where functional cooperation is present and measurable. It may be strongest in economic and technical domains and weaker in security domains.

Limitations: the framework risks conflating pragmatic statecraft with functionalist doctrine; it faces difficulty distinguishing mutual benefit from strategic leverage; Western theoretical concepts may not fully capture Chinese practice; power asymmetry may undermine functionalist optimism.

Falsifying evidence: the framework would be challenged if functional cooperation consistently failed to produce interdependence or institutional binding, if cooperation proved purely extractive with no mutual gains, or if smaller states systematically resisted or exited cooperation.

### 11. Competing Explanations and Theoretical Limitations

The proposed framework must be compared against established alternatives. Liberal intergovernmentalism may better explain China-ASEAN cooperation as state-driven bargaining. Economic statecraft may better capture the strategic use of economic instruments. Complex interdependence may illuminate multiple channels but understate coercion. Chinese IR theories offer civilizational framing but limited empirical testability.

Two competing formulations are under consideration. Formulation A, modified functionalism, treats Chinese Functionalism as a modification of classical functionalism under state primacy. Formulation B, analytical adaptation, treats it as a heuristic for identifying functional cooperation patterns. Formulation B is the provisional working direction, but neither has been conclusively established. The project remains open to the possibility that established theories explain the evidence more convincingly.

### 12. Small-State Agency and Regional Responses

Smaller states do not merely receive China's functional cooperation; they negotiate, resist, and redirect it. Responses vary by state capacity, economic structure, and security interests. Malaysia and Singapore have been described as hedging, combining engagement with insurance (Kuik, 2008). Vietnam cooperates while resisting on maritime issues. Indonesia renegotiates terms. Laos faces high dependence with limited leverage. Cambodia shows close alignment. The Philippines combines economic engagement with maritime resistance and alliance politics.

Smaller-state agency is a key test for the proposed framework. If cooperation is purely top-down, the functionalist emphasis on mutual needs is weakened. The variation in responses suggests that power asymmetry shapes outcomes but does not eliminate agency.

## Part IV: The Philippine Perspective

### 13. The Philippines in China-ASEAN Relations

The Philippines should not be reduced to a simple United States-China rivalry narrative. Philippine foreign policy reflects multiple objectives, including economic development, maritime security, and regional stability. The country participates in ASEAN mechanisms while maintaining security partnerships, including with the United States.

### 14. Economic Cooperation and Maritime Competition

Functional cooperation with China in trade, infrastructure, and disaster management coexists with competition in maritime domains. This coexistence of cooperation and competition is precisely the pattern that a functionalist framework must account for rather than assume away. Economic engagement does not automatically resolve security competition, consistent with the limitations of classical functionalism identified earlier.

### 15. Strategic Autonomy and the Limits of Functional Cooperation

The Philippine case illustrates how smaller states attempt to maintain strategic autonomy amid asymmetry. Economic engagement with China offers development benefits, while maritime disputes and security concerns limit the depth of cooperation. Policy varies across administrations, reflecting domestic political considerations. Functional cooperation and strategic competition constrain each other, and the conditions under which one overrides the other remain an open question.

## Part V: Implications

### 16. The Analytical Value of Chinese Functionalism

The provisional value of the proposed framework lies in its ability to direct attention to sectoral cooperation, institutional engagement, and power asymmetry simultaneously. Whether it offers explanatory advantages over established theories remains to be tested through systematic case studies and falsification analysis.

### 17. Limitations, Counterarguments, and Future Research

This manuscript is based on metadata verified sources, many of which have not undergone full-text review. Several citations require verification. The quantitative data cited from official documents should be independently confirmed. The proposed framework is provisional and has not been tested against systematic empirical evidence.

Future directions include full-text reviews of all cited sources, case studies of specific cooperation mechanisms, testing of observable indicators against empirical data, deeper examination of the Philippine case, and refinement of the theoretical framework based on evidence and collaborator feedback.
