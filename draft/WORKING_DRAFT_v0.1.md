## Introduction: The Problem of Chinese Functionalism

The question of how to characterize China's expanding international engagement has become one of the central problems in contemporary international relations scholarship. Over the past two decades, China has deepened its participation in global and regional institutions, expanded trade and investment flows, promoted infrastructure connectivity, and advanced a wide range of sectoral cooperation initiatives. These developments are well documented, yet their theoretical meaning remains contested. Are they expressions of a coherent strategic doctrine, pragmatic adaptations to interdependence, or evidence of an emerging alternative approach to international order? This manuscript investigates whether the concept of Chinese Functionalism can serve as a useful analytical framework for understanding these developments.

The term Chinese Functionalism is not an established concept in the international relations literature. It is a provisional label for a possible framework that the research team is developing and testing against the evidence. The project does not assume that China pursues a functionalist foreign policy in the classical sense. Rather, it asks whether functional cooperation, understood as sectoral, technical, and institutional collaboration across borders, can help explain patterns in China's international behavior, and whether such an explanation adds analytical value beyond existing theories.

This distinction matters. The existence of functional cooperation between China and other states does not by itself demonstrate the existence of a functionalist doctrine. Functional cooperation can arise from pragmatic calculation, institutional habit, economic self-interest, or strategic design. The task of this manuscript is to investigate these possibilities critically rather than to confirm a predetermined thesis.

The manuscript is organized into five parts. Part I examines the theoretical foundations of functionalism, from Mitrany's classical formulation to neofunctionalism and its critics. Part II analyzes China's practice of functional cooperation, with particular attention to China ASEAN relations. Part III develops and critically evaluates the proposed Chinese Functionalism framework. Part IV considers regional implications, power asymmetry, and the experience of smaller states, including the Philippines. The closing section presents provisional findings, limitations, and directions for further research.

## Part I: Theoretical Foundations of Functionalism

### 1. Classical Functionalism

David Mitrany is commonly regarded as the originator of the functional approach to international organization. Writing during the interwar period and the Second World War, Mitrany proposed that international peace and cooperation could be advanced not through comprehensive political federation but through function specific agencies addressing concrete human needs. His central argument was that the functional approach emphasizes the common index of need rather than the individual index of power (Mitrany, 1948).

Mitrany's vision rested on several key ideas. First, many needs cut across national boundaries, and joint government could be provided for them without requiring full political union. Second, functional arrangements are complementary rather than mutually exclusive, allowing varied membership and autonomous development. Third, the nature of each function determines the scope and powers required for its effective performance, a principle Mitrany called technical self-determination. Fourth, functional cooperation pools only so much sovereignty as is needed for a particular task, thereby mitigating the problem of equal sovereignty among states (Mitrany, 1943; Mitrany, 1948).

Mitrany's approach was also anti statist in orientation. He regarded nationalism as a primary cause of political evils and believed that international organizations should undermine the state as the sole form of social organization by diffusing political power across functional agencies. However, later scholarship has cautioned against treating Mitrany as a founding father in a simple sense. His work drew on earlier technocratic and socialist traditions, including the ideas of Leonard Woolf and G. D. H. Cole, and he functioned more as a catalyst who combined pragmatic and utopian elements into a new synthesis (Mitrany and the Emergence of the Functional Approach, 2017).

The classical functionalist framework carries significant limitations. It assumes that technical tasks can be separated from politics, an assumption that subsequent scholarship has challenged. It tends to underestimate power asymmetry and the strategic interests of states. It also offers limited guidance for situations where functional cooperation intersects with territorial disputes or security competition.

### 2. Neofunctionalism and Regional Integration

Ernst B. Haas developed neofunctionalism as a modification of classical functionalism, applying it specifically to the process of regional integration. In *The Uniting of Europe* (1958), Haas argued that integration proceeds through a dynamic of spillover, in which cooperation in one sector generates pressures for cooperation in related sectors, ultimately leading to political integration (Haas, 1958).

Neofunctionalism identifies several mechanisms. Technical or functional spillover occurs when cooperation in one area creates functional demands for cooperation in adjacent areas. Political spillover occurs when supranational officials act as entrepreneurs, expanding the scope of integration. Cultivated spillover involves the mobilization of interest groups and citizens around regional solutions. Haas also introduced the concept of spillback, acknowledging that integration can reverse (Haas, 1964).

The neofunctionalist thesis was ambitious. It predicted that economic interdependence and functional pressures would lead, through incremental steps, to a supranational political community. Critics argued that this prediction was not borne out by events, particularly the stagnation of European integration in the 1960s and 1970s. Andrew Moravcsik contended that neofunctionalism was over ambitious, one sided, and essentially unfalsifiable, because it sought to explain long term change without micro foundational theories of preferences, bargaining, and institutional delegation (Moravcsik, 2005, as cited in the neofunctionalism literature).

Haas himself contributed to the decline of interest in his own theory by declaring neofunctionalism obsolescent in the early 1970s. Nevertheless, later scholarship has argued that the neofunctionalist conceptual repertoire, including spillover and task expansion, remains analytically productive for understanding European and comparative regional integration (Ruggie, 2005).

### 3. Comparison with Related Theories

Functionalism and neofunctionalism must be distinguished from several related approaches. Liberal institutionalism emphasizes how institutions reduce transaction costs and facilitate cooperation under anarchy, but it tends to assume shared interests and pays less attention to power asymmetry. Constructivism focuses on norms, identities, and socialization, which may underweight material power. Complex interdependence highlights multiple channels of contact and the minor role of military force, yet it can understate coercion and security competition.

These distinctions are important for the Chinese Functionalism project. If the proposed framework merely restates insights from liberal institutionalism or complex interdependence, it adds little. Its potential contribution lies in centering power asymmetry, strategic interests, and the experience of smaller states within a functionalist vocabulary.

## Part II: China and the Practice of Functional Cooperation

### 4. China ASEAN Cooperation as Functional Practice

China's engagement with ASEAN provides the most developed empirical context for examining functional cooperation. The relationship has evolved from dialogue partnership to comprehensive strategic partnership, with cooperation spanning trade, investment, infrastructure, digital connectivity, energy, health, education, and maritime affairs (ASEAN Secretariat, 2024; ASEAN Secretariat, 2025).

Trade and investment constitute the most quantifiable dimension. In 2025, two way merchandise trade between ASEAN and China reached USD 913.1 billion, and Chinese foreign direct investment into ASEAN amounted to USD 26.2 billion (ASEAN Secretariat, 2026). China has been ASEAN's largest trading partner for eighteen consecutive years. These figures indicate deep economic interdependence, though they do not by themselves demonstrate functionalist intent.

Institutional mechanisms have proliferated. The ASEAN China Free Trade Area, upgraded to ACFTA 3.0 with commitments in digital economy, green economy, supply chain connectivity, and MSME development, represents a significant institutional achievement (ASEAN Secretariat, 2024). The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership further deepens regional economic integration. The Plan of Action 2026 to 2030 outlines sectoral priorities including trade, connectivity, digital transformation, energy, and people to people exchanges (ASEAN Secretariat, 2025).

Infrastructure connectivity illustrates the functional dimension clearly. Projects such as the China Laos Railway and the Jakarta Bandung High Speed Railway address concrete connectivity deficits in Southeast Asia. China's comparative advantages in infrastructure construction, combined with ASEAN's capital and technology gaps, create conditions for functional cooperation (Li and Zhang, 2021). The Master Plan on ASEAN Connectivity and China's Belt and Road Initiative have been synergized through joint statements (ASEAN Secretariat, 2026).

### 5. Functional Cooperation and Strategic Interests

The relationship between functional cooperation and strategic interests is the central analytical problem. Functional cooperation can serve multiple purposes simultaneously. It can address genuine welfare needs, create economic interdependence, build institutional habits, and generate political leverage. Distinguishing these motives empirically is difficult.

Critical scholarship has highlighted that China's infrastructure and connectivity initiatives may serve strategic goals, including expanding regional influence and reducing dependence on Western led institutions. The Belt and Road Initiative has been described as contributing to China's strategic goal of expanding its influence in the region through connectivity and cooperation (Springer, 2024). At the same time, ASEAN members have generally welcomed infrastructure investment while seeking to maintain autonomy and avoid excessive dependence.

This tension between cooperation and asymmetry is not unique to the China ASEAN relationship. It reflects a broader pattern in which functional cooperation between unequal partners generates both mutual benefits and distributional concerns. Smaller states have developed strategies to navigate this asymmetry, including hedging, which combines engagement with insurance against potential risks (Kuik, 2008).

## Part III: Chinese Functionalism as a Potential Analytical Framework

### 6. Does Chinese Functionalism Exist as a Concept?

The search for explicitly Chinese functionalist theory in the international relations literature yields limited results. Chinese IR scholarship has developed several indigenous theoretical frameworks, including moral realism (Yan Xuetong), relational theory (Qin Yaqing), Tianxia theory (Zhao Tingyang), and social symbiosis theory (Ren Xiao). These frameworks draw on Chinese philosophical traditions and address questions of order, morality, and relationality, but none is explicitly functionalist in the Mitrany or Haas sense (He, 2015; Chinese School literature, 2024).

The broader debate on constructing international relations theory with Chinese characteristics has been driven by factors including the dominance of Western models, the desire to revive classical Chinese thought, and China's rising global status (He, 2015). This debate has both academic and policy dimensions, and the two should not be conflated. Academic meta theoretical reflection differs from policy oriented concept production designed to frame China's foreign policy (He, 2015).

The implication for this project is clear. Chinese Functionalism is not an established academic theory. It is a proposed analytical framework that the research team must develop and test. The project must distinguish between the existence of functional cooperation in China's practice and the existence of a functionalist foreign policy doctrine. The former is well documented. The latter remains an open question.

### 7. Proposed Framework: Chinese Functionalism

The following framework is provisional and subject to revision based on evidence and human review.

**Theoretical assumptions.** States pursue cooperation in functional domains where mutual gains are identifiable. Functional cooperation can generate interdependence and institutional habits. Power asymmetry shapes who benefits and who bears costs. Cooperation may serve strategic as well as welfare objectives.

**Mechanisms.** Sectoral cooperation in trade, infrastructure, technology, and health. Institutional engagement through ASEAN plus one, RCEP, ACFTA, and the Belt and Road Initiative. Interdependence creation. Norm and standard diffusion. Capacity building and development finance.

**Observable indicators.** Scope and depth of sectoral agreements. Institutional participation and leadership roles. Trade and investment flows. Infrastructure project portfolios. Frequency and coverage of cooperation mechanisms. Smaller state policy responses, including hedging, bandwagoning, and resistance.

**Scope conditions.** The framework applies where functional cooperation is present and measurable. It may be strongest in economic and technical domains and weaker in security domains.

**Limitations.** The framework risks conflating pragmatic statecraft with functionalist doctrine. It faces difficulty distinguishing mutual benefit from strategic leverage. Western theoretical concepts may not fully capture Chinese practice. Power asymmetry may undermine functionalist optimism.

**Falsifying evidence.** The framework would be challenged if functional cooperation consistently failed to produce interdependence or institutional binding, if cooperation proved purely extractive with no mutual gains, or if smaller states systematically resisted or exited cooperation.

**Distinction from existing theories.** Unlike classical functionalism, the proposed framework does not assume de politicization or anti statism. Unlike neofunctionalism, it does not predict automatic political integration. Unlike liberal institutionalism, it centers power asymmetry and strategic interests. Unlike Chinese IR theories, it is practice grounded and sectoral rather than civilizational.

## Part IV: Regional Implications and the Southeast Asian Experience

### 8. ASEAN, Regional Order, and Power Asymmetry

ASEAN's institutional approach to regional order emphasizes consensus, non interference, and incremental cooperation. This approach has enabled sustained engagement with China despite significant power asymmetry. The ASEAN Outlook on the Indo Pacific and the various ASEAN China mechanisms reflect an attempt to manage great power competition through institutionalized dialogue and functional cooperation.

However, the relationship is not without tension. Maritime disputes in the South China Sea illustrate the limits of functional cooperation when core sovereignty interests are at stake. Functional cooperation in trade and infrastructure does not automatically resolve security competition. This observation is consistent with the limitations of classical functionalism identified earlier.

### 9. Smaller States and the Philippine Experience

Smaller states in Southeast Asia face the challenge of cooperating with a larger and more powerful neighbor while preserving autonomy. The Philippine experience is particularly instructive because it combines deep economic engagement with China, ongoing maritime disputes, and a security alliance with the United States.

The Philippine case should not be reduced to a simple US China rivalry narrative. Philippine foreign policy reflects multiple objectives, including economic development, maritime security, and regional stability. Functional cooperation with China in areas such as trade, infrastructure, and disaster management coexists with competition in maritime domains. This coexistence of cooperation and competition is precisely the pattern that a functionalist framework must account for rather than assume away.

The concept of hedging captures part of this dynamic. Smaller states engage functionally with China while maintaining security insurance through other partnerships. Whether this hedging stabilizes or destabilizes the region depends on context and is an empirical question rather than a theoretical given.

## Closing: Implications, Limitations, and Future Directions

### Provisional Findings

1. Classical functionalism and neofunctionalism provide useful concepts for understanding sectoral cooperation and integration, but their assumptions about de politicization and automatic spillover are not fully supported by the Chinese case.
2. China's engagement with ASEAN exhibits extensive functional cooperation across multiple sectors, documented in official agreements and trade statistics.
3. The relationship between functional cooperation and strategic interests remains analytically unresolved. Cooperation and competition coexist.
4. Chinese Functionalism is not an established theory. It is a proposed framework whose value depends on its ability to generate testable hypotheses and distinguish itself from existing approaches.
5. Smaller states navigate asymmetry through hedging and institutional engagement, suggesting that functional cooperation does not eliminate power politics.

### Limitations

This manuscript is based on metadata verified sources, many of which have not undergone full text review. Several citations require verification. The quantitative data cited from official documents should be independently confirmed. The proposed framework is provisional and has not been tested against systematic empirical evidence.

### Future Directions

1. Conduct full text reviews of all cited sources.
2. Develop case studies of specific functional cooperation mechanisms, such as ACFTA 3.0, the China Laos Railway, and digital connectivity initiatives.
3. Test the framework's observable indicators against empirical data.
4. Examine the Philippine case in greater depth.
5. Refine the theoretical framework based on evidence and collaborator feedback.
6. Determine the final citation style and chapter architecture through human review.
